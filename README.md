# Morocco-Mens-National-Team-Tactical-Identity-Across-Three-Matches

A qualitative analysis of Morocco’s tactical identity across three matches: Jordan (2021), Paraguay (2026) and Spain (2018). Using manual video observation with timestamps, I examine build-up, pressing, defensive shape and transitions, and how Morocco adapts to each opponent while keeping its core habits.

## 1. Project Overview

### What I set out to do

I wanted to understand how Morocco plays when they build from the back, defend, win the ball and lose it, and how those things change from one opponent to the next. I watched three matches closely and wrote down what I saw, with timestamps, so that every point in this project can be traced back to a moment in a game.

### The question

How does Morocco's way of playing change across different opponents and match situations, while still keeping some of the same basic habits?

### Scope

This project covers three international matches. For each one I looked at team shape, how Morocco keep the ball and move it forward, how they defend, how they press, what they do straight after winning or losing the ball, and the decisions their attackers make.

I don't treat a team's identity as one fixed formation. A team can keep the same habits while changing shape depending on where the opponent stands, how much space is available and what the game needs at that moment. That is the idea I follow throughout.

## 2. How I Did It

I watched each match and noted down specific moments. I then sorted every moment into one of four phases of play:

- **In possession:** how Morocco build up, circulate the ball, use width and make decisions in the final third.
- **Out of possession:** how they set up to defend, how compact they are, how they press and how they protect the space behind the defence.
- **Attacking transitions:** what they do right after winning the ball.
- **Defensive transitions:** what they do right after losing the ball.

For each moment I kept three things apart: what happened, what I think it shows about how Morocco play, and where it happened (match and timestamp). Keeping these separate stops me from claiming more than a moment can show. One moment is one moment. I only call something a pattern when I saw it in more than one match, and I only call something a habit within a match when my notes describe it happening repeatedly.

This is a written, observation-based study. It describes what Morocco did in these three games. It doesn't use event data or statistics, so I don't give percentages or success rates anywhere.

## 4. Match-by-Match Analysis

### 4.1 Morocco vs Jordan (FIFA Arab Cup 2021)

#### Build-up and moving the ball forward

Morocco started several attacks from the back. In one sequence a central midfielder passed to a centre-back, who played it to the right-back, took it back, and then moved it on towards a midfielder. They also played longer passes into Jordan's half, combined in short passes between midfielders and forwards, and tried to use the wide areas.

This tells me Morocco didn't rely on one way of moving the ball up the pitch. They would pass around the back to keep the ball while looking for a way forward, or go longer when they wanted to get forward quickly.

#### Shape

Morocco's shape changed during the game. Some moments looked like a 4-4-2. At around 30:00, a full-back dropped into the back line while others recovered, which made the team look more like a 5-3-2. At times the defensive line also pushed up towards the halfway line.

So the 5-3-2 was a temporary shape, not Morocco's fixed formation. It appeared as players recovered towards their own goal. The higher line shows Morocco trying to play further up the pitch, which keeps the team closer together, but it also leaves space behind the defenders.

#### Decisions in the final third

Three moments stood out:

- **27:45:** The player on the ball shot under pressure while a teammate was running towards the penalty area. The goalkeeper dealt with the shot.
- **32:14:** The player on the ball shot over the goal while teammates were in advanced positions.
- **71:19:** The player on the ball shot towards the goalkeeper while a teammate on the right had space and others were moving into the box.

In all three, a teammate was in a position to receive a pass. These are the moments where the choice between shooting and passing mattered most. I'm not saying a pass would definitely have been better each time. I'm pointing out that in these three moments a teammate was available and the player chose to shoot.

#### Defending

When Jordan had the ball, Morocco pressed around the ball while players recovered into defensive positions. Midfielders and forwards helped close down opponents and reduce space. At times the back line dropped deeper and the team became more compact. At around 30:00 came the 5-3-2 shape described above. At around 30:50, midfielders followed opposing players closely to make it harder for them to receive the ball and turn.

Morocco's defending here combined pressure on the ball with getting back into a solid shape.

#### Attacking transitions

After winning the ball, Morocco tried to use the wide areas, look for forward passes and get towards Jordan's goal. Three moments show different results:

- **2:16:** Morocco moved forward on the counter, but a foul stopped the attack.
- **3:08 to 3:12:** An attacker went forward even though he was outnumbered 1v3. The move ended in a goal after an assist.
- **36:31:** Morocco counter-attacked towards goal, but the goalkeeper collected the ball.

The 3:08 sequence is a clear example of a player driving an attack forward against the odds and it ending in a goal. The other two show that a counter-attack can start well and still end without a chance.

#### Defensive transitions

When Morocco lost the ball, nearby players pressed straight away while teammates ran back into defensive positions. This pressing sometimes won the ball back, and Morocco then reorganised. This looks like an attempt to win the ball back immediately, with teammates recovering behind the press in case it didn't work.

#### Forcing play wide

At around 53:53, Morocco pushed Jordan's attack wide and the sequence ended with a Jordan corner. This shows one way an attack of that kind ended. The corner on its own doesn't tell me whether the defending was good or bad.

#### Key points from the Jordan match

1. Morocco moved the ball forward in several ways: short passes, circulation across the back and longer balls.
2. Their defensive shape changed, with 4-4-2 and temporary 5-3-2 looks.
3. They pressed and recovered at the same time.
4. Their counters ended in different ways: a goal, a foul and a goalkeeper save.
5. Three shots came while teammates were in positions to receive a pass.
6. A higher defensive line brought Morocco further up the pitch but left space behind it.

### 4.2 Morocco vs Paraguay (International Friendly, 2026)

Morocco won 2–1. Morocco started in a 4-2-3-1 and Paraguay in a 4-4-2.

#### Keeping the ball and building up

Morocco built through the middle, with a midfielder passing to a centre-back. In one sequence the centre-back tried to find the striker, but the ball was contested and came back to a Moroccan player. When going forward was difficult, Morocco played out to a wide midfielder or went back to the defenders and tried a different route. The defenders passed the ball among themselves, looking for teammates in the middle and out wide. My notes describe this as happening repeatedly whenever the direct route was blocked.

So when the way forward was closed, Morocco didn't force it. They kept the ball and changed where they were trying to go.

#### Using width and support

Morocco looked calm under pressure and used short passes to keep the ball. Players spread into wide areas to give the ball carrier options. When a route was blocked, the ball was sometimes passed backwards before Morocco tried another way. In some attacking phases the defensive line moved up into Paraguay's half, with midfielders and forwards spreading out to support.

The best example is the sequence at **52:42**. After winning the ball, Morocco moved forward down a wide area while a teammate ran through the centre and two more entered the penalty area. The ball carrier reached the corner of the box despite the defensive line recovering. He shot, and a teammate finished for Morocco's second goal.

This goal came from a wide attack with players in the middle and in the box. The ball carrier had several teammates to link with, which is what made the move work.

#### Defending

After losing the ball, Morocco recovered into defensive positions. The back line changed its depth depending on where the ball was, sometimes holding a medium block and sometimes dropping into a low block in their own half. Midfielders and forwards moved into central areas and pressed to limit Paraguay's time and space. My notes describe this as happening throughout the match.

Morocco didn't defend at one fixed height. They moved between a medium and a low block as the situation changed.

#### The defensive line and protecting the goal

At **1:43**, Morocco's defensive line was up in Paraguay's half with space behind it. That would have been a risk if Morocco had lost the ball, but Paraguay didn't make use of the space on that occasion.

At around **71:00**, the goalkeeper came away from his normal position to deal with a Paraguay attack. The ball slipped from his hands, which gave the Paraguay striker a chance to score. A Moroccan centre-back got to the striker quickly, put him under pressure and stopped the ball going in. The ball then went out of play.

The first moment shows the trade-off of a high line. The second is a good individual defensive action that saved a goal.

#### Attacking transitions

Two moments show different results after Morocco won the ball:

- **21:35 to 21:40:** Morocco lost the ball at about 21:35 and won it back around five seconds later. They tried to spread out and move forward, but the pass to a wide midfielder failed under pressure and the ball went out of play.
- **52:42:** Morocco won the ball and attacked down a wide area with support through the middle and runners into the box. It ended in the second goal.

Winning the ball quickly doesn't guarantee a good attack. At 21:35 Morocco got the ball back fast but the next pass went wrong. At 52:42 they turned a regain into a goal.

#### Defensive transitions

After losing the ball, the back line recovered towards a medium or low block, and midfielders and forwards moved into central areas and pressed the ball right away. At 21:35 Morocco lost the ball and won it back about five seconds later, which is a good example of getting it back quickly.

#### Paraguay's long passes

Paraguay used longer passes to get past Morocco's central pressure. That gave Morocco a different defensive job: the back line's position, winning the first ball and being organised for the second ball all mattered.

#### Key points from the Paraguay match

1. Morocco went back to the defenders to recycle the ball when the direct route was blocked.
2. They used both central combinations and wide areas to move forward.
3. The goal at 52:42 combined wide play, a central runner and players entering the box.
4. They moved between medium and low defensive blocks.
5. They won the ball back in about five seconds at 21:35.
6. The high line at 1:43 left space behind it.
7. A centre-back stopped a likely goal at around 71:00.

### 4.3 Morocco vs Spain (FIFA World Cup 2018)

The match finished 2–2. Both teams started in a 4-2-3-1.

#### Attacking after winning the ball

Morocco tried to attack once they had won the ball, especially when Spain's attacking shape left space. They tried to move quickly, use the space and link up with short passes on their way towards Spain's goal.

At **13:17**, a Moroccan attacker picked up the ball on a counter-attack, got past two opponents and went towards goal before finishing. Spain's defensive line was around the middle of the pitch, and no teammate was close to support him. He kept going at speed and scored Morocco's first goal.

This was a clear case of Morocco punishing space with a fast individual run. It doesn't mean Morocco only attack this way. It shows what one player could do when the space was there.

#### Staying compact

In the first half, Morocco focused on defending. They generally sat in a medium-to-low block, got back into their own half and kept tight distances. Midfielders and the forward dropped back when Spain advanced, which cut down the space in the middle. Spain still had a lot of the ball.

In the second half there were moments when Morocco were more willing to spread out, engage opponents and look to go forward, before returning to a more cautious shape. At **69:36 to 69:39**, Morocco looked to be in a 5-4-1 defensive shape. My notes show this happening once, linked to the situation in the match.

So Morocco's shape changed with the game, and the 5-4-1 was a short-term adjustment rather than a new base formation.

#### Pressing Spain's build-up

At **00:33**, Spain passed from the middle of the pitch back towards their defenders before trying to move into Morocco's half. Three Moroccan players pressed the ball carrier and the nearby passing options. Morocco won the ball but couldn't move forward effectively afterwards.

The pressure worked as a team action, not just a challenge on one player. But winning the ball didn't lead to a good attack here, so the press and the attack that followed have to be judged separately.

#### Key points from the Spain match

1. Morocco defended in a medium-to-low block for long periods of the first half.
2. Three players pressed Spain's build-up at 00:33 and won the ball.
3. The attacker at 13:17 beat two opponents on the way to scoring.
4. That counter-attack ended in Morocco's first goal.
5. The 5-4-1 shape at 69:36 to 69:39 was a one-off change.
6. After the regain at 00:33, Morocco didn't build a good attack.

### 5.1 What stayed the same

**Looking for a way forward.** In all three matches Morocco tried to find a route towards goal. Against Jordan it was short passes, longer balls and wide play. Against Paraguay it was recycling and a wide attack with support. Against Spain it was quick forward play after winning the ball.

**Pressure and recovery.** Against Jordan, nearby players pressed while others recovered and midfielders tracked opponents. Against Paraguay, Morocco pressed around the ball and recovered into medium or low blocks. Against Spain, three players pressed Spain's build-up at 00:33.

**Changing defensive shape.** There was no single shape. Against Jordan, 4-4-2 and 5-3-2. Against Paraguay, medium and low blocks. Against Spain, a medium-to-low block and a 5-4-1 moment.

**Winning the ball is only step one.** Each match has a regain that led nowhere and one that led to a goal. Against Jordan, the counter at 2:16 ended in a foul while the move at 3:08 ended in a goal. Against Paraguay, the regain at 21:35 ended in a failed pass while 52:42 ended in a goal. Against Spain, the regain at 00:33 didn't lead to an attack while 13:17 ended in a goal.

### 5.2 What was different

Against Jordan, my notes are mostly about the different ways Morocco moved the ball, the changes of shape and the shooting decisions. Against Paraguay, they are about keeping the ball and the wide attack at 52:42. Against Spain, they are about staying compact and the counter-attack goal at 13:17.

These are differences in what I saw in each game. I'm not claiming Morocco played three completely different ways, or that the opponent alone caused each difference.

## 6. Habits That Showed Up in More Than One Match

**1. More than one way to move the ball forward.** Short passes, circulation, longer balls, recycling and wide play all appeared across the three games. Morocco could keep the ball or go direct, depending on what was in front of them.

**2. Pressing and recovering together.** In every match, Morocco pressed around the ball while players got back into position. Pressure and recovery worked as a pair.

**3. Changing defensive shape and height.** Temporary 5-3-2 and 5-4-1 shapes, a 4-4-2 look, and shifts between medium and low blocks all appeared. No single shape stood for Morocco's defending.

**4. Using space when they won the ball.** Goals came in every match: an outnumbered attacker driving forward against Jordan, a wide move with a central runner and players in the box against Paraguay, and a fast individual run against Spain.

**5. A regain and a good attack are two different things.** In every match there was a regain that did not become a good attack, next to one that became a goal.

**6. A high line leaves space behind.** Against Jordan and Paraguay the line moved up the pitch, which put Morocco in a strong position but left space behind the defenders.

## 7. How Morocco Looked in Each Match

**Against Jordan**, Morocco moved the ball forward in different ways and changed shape as they defended. The goal at 3:08 showed an attacker going forward against the odds. The three shots at 27:45, 32:14 and 71:19 were moments where a teammate was available.

**Against Paraguay**, Morocco kept the ball and changed routes when one was blocked. The goal at 52:42 came from a wide attack with central support. The back line moved between medium and low blocks, and the high line at 1:43 showed the risk of space behind.

**Against Spain**, Morocco defended compactly for long periods and pressed Spain's build-up at 00:33. They scored from a quick counter at 13:17 and briefly changed to a 5-4-1 late on.

Across the three games Morocco kept doing the same kinds of things, which are finding a way forward, pressing and recovering, and using space, while the way they did them changed with the situation. I can't say from my notes whether those changes were planned by the coaches. What I can say is what I saw happen on the pitch.

## 9. Areas to Work On

**Shooting or passing in the final third.** Against Jordan at 27:45, 32:14 and 71:19, the player on the ball shot while a teammate was running, in an advanced position or in space on the right.

**Space behind a high line.** Against Jordan the line moved towards halfway. Against Paraguay at 1:43 it was in the opponent's half with space behind. Paraguay didn't use that space in that moment, but it shows the cost of playing high.

**What happens right after a regain.** Jordan 2:16 (foul), Jordan 36:31 (goalkeeper collected), Paraguay 21:35 to 21:40 (failed pass) and Spain 00:33 (no good attack) all show Morocco winning or carrying the ball without turning it into a good chance. The goals at 3:08, 52:42 and 13:17 show it can work, so this is about doing it more often.

**The goalkeeper moment at 71:00.** When the goalkeeper left his normal position and the ball slipped, Paraguay's striker got a chance. A centre-back saved Morocco, but the situation shows how fast that kind of moment can turn dangerous.

**The corner at 53:53 against Jordan.** Morocco forced play wide, and the sequence still ended in a corner. A corner doesn't mean the defending was poor, but it shows how that kind of attack finished.

## 11. Morocco's Tactical Identity in These Three Matches

Four ideas describe how Morocco played in these games.

**Flexible ball progression.** Short passing, recycling, longer balls and wide attacks all appeared. Morocco had more than one way to move forward.

**Defending with pressure and recovery.** Morocco pressed, tracked opponents and got back into position, and these two things worked together.

**Changing shape as the game changed.** Temporary 5-3-2 and 5-4-1 shapes, a 4-4-2 look and shifts between medium and low blocks all showed up. No single shape defined their defending.

**Using space to attack.** In each match Morocco scored after moving forward into space, in a different way each time.

Put together, Morocco's identity in these matches is about having several ways to move forward, pressing the ball while recovering into shape, changing shape when needed, and using space when it opens.

## 12. Conclusion

I studied Morocco's play in three matches: Jordan in the 2021 Arab Cup, Paraguay in a 2026 friendly and Spain in the 2018 World Cup.

A few things showed up again and again. Morocco moved the ball forward in different ways, from short combinations and recycling to longer balls and wide attacks. They pressed opponents, tracked players and recovered into organised shapes. Their defensive shape changed from game to game, from 4-4-2 and 5-3-2 against Jordan, to medium and low blocks against Paraguay, to a medium-to-low block and a short 5-4-1 against Spain.

They scored in different ways too: an attacker driving forward against Jordan at 3:08, a wide attack with central support against Paraguay at 52:42, and a fast individual run against Spain at 13:17.

There were weaker moments as well. Three shots against Jordan came when a teammate was available. A high defensive line left space behind it against Jordan and Paraguay. And several regains did not turn into good attacks.

My answer to the research question is this: in these three games Morocco changed their shape, their build-up and their way of attacking to fit the situation, while keeping the same core habits of finding a route forward, pressing and recovering, and using available space.

## 15. Where the Footage Came From and How I Handled It

### 15.1 Why I used YouTube footage

I built this project from match footage that anyone can watch on YouTube. I did this because I couldn't get authorised full-match recordings, I didn't have permission to use official footage, and I couldn't afford a professional video-analysis platform.

I'm an independent, aspiring football performance analyst based in Tanzania, and getting official recordings and professional tools was both hard and expensive for me. Publicly available videos gave me a practical way to start building my manual observation skills and my tactical analysis portfolio.

I want to be clear about what this choice means. I used YouTube because it was the footage I could reach. I'm not saying these videos are the same as official analyst footage, and I'm not saying that being able to watch a video gives me the right to download, edit, share or publish it. I carried on with what I had while I kept trying to get proper footage and tools.

### 15.2 Asking the Royal Moroccan Football Federation (FRMF)

I contacted the FRMF and asked for help getting match footage for my analysis. I wanted authorised recordings so I could move from watching public broadcasts to a more professional video-analysis workflow.

I sent an initial request and then a follow-up email asking for a reply. When I wrote this up, I still hadn't received a real answer.

So I carried on with the footage I had. I treat the missing reply as a limit on my access, nothing more. I'm not reading it as a refusal of my project or as the federation's view of my work.

### 15.3 Asking CAF and PSG Media

I also looked for other official routes. I contacted the Confederation of African Football (CAF) to ask for help getting match footage and the permission or licence to use it. I contacted PSG Media with the same request for footage and licensing help.

I hoped these organisations might make suitable recordings available so I wouldn't have to depend on public broadcast videos. At the time of writing, neither had replied, so the question of authorised footage through these routes is still open and I kept using the YouTube recordings.

I mention these requests because they show I tried to find legitimate ways to get footage, rather than assuming public broadcasts could be reused freely.

### 15.4 Hudl and Wyscout

I also contacted Hudl and Wyscout to find out about their professional video and analysis resources. They replied, but the cost of the packages was more than I could afford. I couldn't go ahead with a paid package.

That limited the resources I had, but it didn't stop the project. I kept going with manual observation, watching the footage I could reach and writing down what I saw about team structure, player movement, transitions and specific moments.

Manual observation is not the same as using a professional platform. I did not use licensed Hudl or Wyscout footage, platform datasets or automated event coding.

### 15.5 Why the clips still have the original commentary

The clips I used still have the original broadcast commentary. The source videos were broadcasts that included both the match and the commentators. When I prepared the clips with my tactical annotations, the original audio stayed in.

I thought about removing the commentary, but I decided not to change the audio. I don't have a confirmed licence that allows me to modify it, and the permissions question is still open. So the annotated clips keep the commentary from the source recordings.

This was a careful choice while the rights are unclear. It isn't a legal ruling that removing commentary is always forbidden, since what's allowed depends on the rights, the licence terms and how the footage is used.

I don't use the commentary as evidence of what Morocco were trying to do. My conclusions come from what I saw on the pitch: the actions, the positions, the movement, the passing sequences and the defensive shape.

### 15.6 How this shaped the way I worked

Without authorised analyst footage or an affordable professional package, I worked by watching and writing notes. That is why the project focuses on:

### 15.7 Copyright and sharing

Match footage can be protected by copyright and other restrictions. A video being on a public platform doesn't mean I have permission to download, change, copy or share it.

I contacted FRMF, CAF and PSG Media to look for authorised footage and permission. The reply from Hudl and Wyscout showed me that professional resources exist, even though I couldn't pay for them.

Because I haven't confirmed any permissions, I'm not claiming the recordings or my annotated clips are licensed to be shared freely. If I publish or share any video in the future, I will follow the copyright rules, the platform terms and the licence conditions. Where I can't get permission to show footage, I can share my own written analysis, observation tables and diagrams that I create myself.

My tactical analysis is my own work. The match recordings underneath it belong to other people, and I keep the two separate.
